# Positioning and sequencing

The two decisions that make or break a launch happen before launch day: what you position against, and the order you do things in.

## Position against the real alternative

The buyer is not choosing between your product and a competitor. They are choosing between your product and whatever they do right now, and right now they mostly do nothing or cope with a workaround. The workaround wins by default because it is already working and switching costs effort.

Name the current alternative in plain terms. A team tracking bugs in a shared spreadsheet is not comparing your tool to a rival tracker; they are comparing it to the spreadsheet that already holds their data and that everyone already knows how to use. The spreadsheet is free, familiar, and running. That is the thing to beat.

Include doing nothing as an explicit option, because it is usually the strongest competitor. Doing nothing has no price, no learning curve, and no risk of a failed migration. To beat it, the launch has to make the cost of the status quo visible: the hours lost, the errors made, the deals missed. A launch that lists features answers a question the buyer has not asked yet.

State the switching cost honestly and give a reason large enough to cover it. Moving off a working system costs time, risk, and the discomfort of change. If your advantage is small, the switch will not happen, and the honest move is to find the one situation where the pain of the status quo is sharp enough that the switch pays for itself. Launch to that situation first.

## When the only honest edge is small

If the product is a modest improvement over what people already have, do not manufacture a bigger claim. Instead, narrow the launch to the buyer for whom the small improvement is a big deal. A 10 percent speed gain is nothing to most and everything to the one team whose build time is the bottleneck. Find that team and launch to them.

## Sequencing the launch

A launch is a sequence, not a day. Firing everything at once means a broken signup form or a confusing headline is seen by the whole audience before anyone can fix it.

Warm the closest audience first. The people who already know and trust the maker are the most forgiving of rough edges and the most likely to convert and vouch. Reach them before the strangers, both to catch problems early and to gather the proof strangers will need.

Prerequisites before promotion. Nothing goes wide until the page is live, the signup works end to end, the payment path has been tested with a real transaction, someone is watching the support inbox, and the first users are ready to say something true about the product. A launch that sends traffic to a broken form spends attention that does not come back.

Gather proof in the early window, then widen. The first users produce the testimonials, the screenshots, and the answers to the hard questions. Feed those into the wider push so the strangers arrive to a page that already answers their doubts.

## The prerequisite list

Write it as a checklist and verify each item, not as an assumption. A typical list: the landing page live and matching the announcement, the signup or purchase path tested with a real run, the pricing correct and visible, the support channel staffed for the launch window, the early users briefed, the obvious hard questions answered in writing, and the analytics recording the one metric that defines success. Anything unchecked on this list is a way for the launch to leak the attention it earns.
