# Audience, channels, and the quiet launch

The total market is a vanity number. What matters is who you can reach this month, where their attention already sits, and what you do when the launch lands quietly, which it usually does.

## Reachable audience versus total market

The total addressable market is the count of everyone who could in theory use the product. It is useful for a fundraising slide and useless for planning a launch, because you cannot reach most of it and not this month.

Count the reachable audience instead. Add up the people you can actually put a message in front of during the launch window. An email list of 2000 with a 30 percent open rate reaches about 600 people. A community post that 5000 members could see but that realistically surfaces to a fraction. A follower count where any single post reaches a slice, not the whole. The honest total is usually in the hundreds or low thousands for a first launch, not the millions in the market slide.

Set expectations from the reachable number. If 600 people see the launch and a strong early conversion rate is a few percent, that is a couple dozen signups, not a couple thousand. Writing that down before launch day is what stops a normal result from being experienced as a failure.

If the reachable audience is near zero, the launch is not the task. Building an audience is, and that happens over weeks of showing up where the audience already is, before there is anything to announce. A launch to no one teaches nothing.

## Choosing channels

Rank channels by where the target audience already pays attention, not by reach or by the maker's comfort. A large channel full of the wrong people is worse than a small one full of the right people.

For each candidate channel, ask three things. Do the target buyers actually gather here. Can a message from someone like the maker surface here, or is it pay to play. What are the norms, because the same post can read as useful in one place and as spam in another.

Pick the fewest channels you can execute well. A strong, native presence in the one forum where the audience lives beats a copy-pasted announcement dropped into five. Each channel has its own dialect and its own rules about self-promotion, and honoring them is the difference between welcome and banned.

Respect the community rules literally. Many communities forbid or restrict promotion, and breaking that rule on launch day gets the post removed and the maker distrusted. Where a community forbids direct promotion, the route in is being a genuine participant first, which again is work that predates the launch.

## When the launch lands quietly

The most likely outcome of a first launch is quiet. A handful of signups, a few comments, and then the traffic fades. This is normal and it is not a verdict on the product.

Do not respond by relaunching louder to the same audience. They already saw it. Shouting again spends the remaining goodwill and changes nothing.

Respond by learning. Talk to the few people who did engage: what made them look, what nearly stopped them, what they expected that was not there. A dozen real conversations are worth more than the launch metrics, because they tell you why the rest stayed silent.

Fix one thing and reach a different slice. Sharpen the positioning based on what the engaged few said, then reach a new audience through a different channel or a narrower message. Distribution is a repeated activity, not a single event, and most products that work found their audience on the third or fifth attempt, not the first.

Keep the assets working after launch day. A launch-day post disappears, but a page that ranks, a guide that gets shared, and a community reputation that compounds keep bringing people in long after the announcement. Treat launch day as the start of distribution, not its peak.
