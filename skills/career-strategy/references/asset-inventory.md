# The non-transferable asset inventory

The recommendation is only theirs if it uses something most people in the same position do not have.
This file is the set of prompts that surface those assets, because they rarely appear on a CV and the
user usually undervalues them. Work through each group and write the concrete answer, not the category.

## Domain knowledge from unglamorous work

- Which industries have you seen from the inside, and for how long?
- What do you understand about how that industry actually runs that an outsider would get wrong?
- What vocabulary, regulation, or workflow do you know that a newcomer spends a year learning?
- Where does software or analysis touch that industry, and who already trusts you there?

The pattern that pays: a domain most technical people avoid, plus a skill most people in that domain do
not have. Insurance claims plus programming. Nursing plus data analysis. Trades plus operations.

## Relationships with real weight

- Who would take your call today and spend an hour helping?
- Who has hired you before and would hire you again?
- Who would refer you into their company without being asked twice?
- Which communities or groups know your name and your work?

Count only relationships that would act, not contacts in an address book. A weak tie who will make an
introduction is worth more than a strong tie who will only wish you luck.

## Credentials with gate value

- Which licences or certifications do you hold that legally restrict who may do the work?
- Which ones are practically required even if not legally, meaning nobody gets hired without them?
- Which are expensive or slow to obtain, so that holding one is itself a moat?

A credential that anyone can get in a weekend has no gate value. One that takes years or a supervised
period does.

## Geography and language

- Which markets can you serve because of where you are or what you speak?
- Which time zone makes you reachable to a client base that struggles to find local coverage?
- Which regulatory region do you understand well enough to operate in without a local partner?

## Tolerance for what others avoid

- What can you stand that most people cannot: on-call, heavy travel, direct sales, conflict, regulatory
  detail, long feedback cycles, physical discomfort?
- Where has that tolerance already been rewarded with pay or responsibility?

Tolerance is an asset because it is scarce. The work nobody wants to do pays more per unit of skill.

## Financial position

- How many months can you survive with no income?
- What fixed obligations continue regardless of a career move?
- How much of a pay cut could you absorb, and for how long, to reach a higher ceiling later?

This group decides how much risk any path can carry, so it is filled in first, not last.

## The combination question

Single skills have many suppliers, so value concentrates in combinations. After the groups above are
filled, ask the one question that decides most of the recommendation: what pair or trio do you have that
is rare together? The answer is where the advice should point, because it is the part a competitor cannot
quickly copy.

## Worked example of the swap test

A user asks whether to leave a stable analyst role to learn a new programming framework. The generic
answer is yes, learn the growing skill. The inventory shows eight years inside pharmaceutical supply
chains, a network of former colleagues now at health-tech firms, and twelve months of runway. The real
recommendation is to move toward pharmaceutical supply-chain software using the domain and the network,
not to compete as a generic newcomer in a crowded framework. Swap the person for a fresh graduate with no
domain and no network, and the recommendation collapses, which is how you know it was actually theirs.
