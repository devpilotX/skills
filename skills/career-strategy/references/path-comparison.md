# Comparing two paths with the crossover method

Vague paths cannot be compared, and a list of pros and cons hides the one thing that decides most career
questions: how long until the new path overtakes staying put, and whether the user can survive the gap.
This file gives the method and a worked comparison. Every figure is an assumption to be replaced with the
user's real numbers or retrieved, dated market data.

## Turn each option into three numbers

For every path, get:

- Time to first income at the new level, in months.
- Money required or forgone during that time, meaning tuition, lost pay, and any moving cost.
- Probability it works, estimated from retrieved evidence rather than optimism.

A path with no duration and no cost is a wish. Refuse to compare wishes.

## The crossover point

Plot cumulative earnings for each path over time. Staying put is usually a straight line. A path with an
upfront cost starts below zero, then climbs at a steeper slope once the new income arrives. The month
where the climbing line crosses the straight line is the crossover point.

Two facts matter more than the slope:

- How far below zero the climbing line dips, because that trough is the cash the user must have to reach
  the other side. A correct plan that dips below the user's runway is not affordable, so it is not a plan.
- How many months past today the crossover sits. People routinely assume it is two years when the
  arithmetic says five, and sometimes the lines never cross, which is fine if the reason for the move was
  never money.

## Worked comparison

A user earning 60000 a year is weighing two paths against staying.

Path A, a one-year full-time course, then a role at a higher level.

  Year 1: income drops to 0, tuition 15000. Cumulative versus staying: -75000.
  From year 2: new role pays 85000, so 25000 a year ahead of the 60000 baseline.
  Crossover: 75000 / 25000 = 3 years after finishing, so 4 years from today.
  Trough: -75000, which requires savings or borrowing of at least that to survive.

Path B, staying employed while studying part-time over two years, no tuition break from work.

  Years 1 to 2: income stays 60000, tuition 8000 spread over two years, and less free time.
  From year 3: new role pays 82000, so 22000 a year ahead of baseline.
  Crossover: 16000 / 22000 is under a year after finishing, so about 3 years from today.
  Trough: -8000, well inside most runways.

Path B crosses over sooner and never dips below a survivable level, at the cost of two hard years of
working and studying at once. Path A reaches a slightly higher ceiling and demands 75000 of runway the
user may not have. The recommendation turns on the runway number, which is why the inventory collects it
first.

## Reading the result

- If both paths cross over inside the user's remaining working years and one is clearly affordable, that
  is usually the answer.
- If the higher-ceiling path is unaffordable, say so plainly. An unaffordable optimum is not a real
  option, and pretending otherwise wastes the user's time.
- If neither path crosses over before retirement, the move is not about money, so name what it is about
  and let the user weigh that directly rather than dressing it up as a financial case.
- Age changes the calculation because time is the one input nobody gets back. The same two-year detour
  has a different crossover horizon at 25 than at 50, and the model should say so rather than treating
  every user as having unlimited years ahead.

## What this method does not settle

The crossover is a money comparison. It does not weigh how much the user will enjoy the work, how the
choice affects people who depend on them, or how it interacts with health. Compute the crossover to make
the money honest, then set it beside the non-financial factors rather than letting it stand in for them.
