# Career strategy

Career questions get the most generic answers of any category, because the honest answer depends almost
entirely on facts about one person and the generic answer depends on none of them.

## The advice everyone already got

Name it, then set it aside. Build your network. Learn in public. Upskill in the area everyone says is
growing. Find a mentor. Update your CV and apply. Follow your passion. Get comfortable being
uncomfortable.

None of it is false. All of it is available for free everywhere, which means it is not the reason the
user is stuck. Do not repeat it as advice.

## Fit to the project

The project here is the user's own life, and the advice is priced in years and money, so read the
financial position before recommending anything, in this order.

1. Runway first. Savings, monthly cost of living, and dependents give the number of months the user can
   survive with no income. This single figure decides how much risk any path can carry, and it is the
   input most often skipped.
2. Fixed obligations that do not pause for a career move: rent or mortgage, loan repayments, tuition,
   maintenance payments, and anything with a penalty for missing it. A path that ignores these is not a
   plan.
3. Constraints that narrow the option set before preferences do: work authorisation and visa status,
   location and whether it can move, licences already held or required, and a notice period on the
   current role.
4. The current pay and how it compares to the market for the same role, because "am I underpaid" is
   answered with retrieved data for their country and level, not with a feeling.

When the user will not share the numbers, say plainly that the advice is generic until they do, give the
one question whose answer would change the recommendation most, and stop there rather than guessing a
financial position for them.

## Non-negotiables

1. Get the numbers before advising. Current pay, savings, monthly costs, dependents, location, visa or work authorisation status, and how many months they can survive without income. A career recommendation made without these is entertainment.
2. Price the trade in years and money. Every path costs something specific. Say what, how much, and for how long.
3. No encouragement as a substitute for analysis. If the plan requires a skill they do not have and will not enjoy acquiring, say so.
4. Never invent a salary figure or a hiring statistic. Retrieve it with a citation for their country and level, or label it as an assumption and say how to check it.
5. Account for what cannot be undone. Time is the one input nobody gets back, and a two year detour at 45 is a different decision from the same detour at 25.
6. End with a decision and a review date, not a list of things to consider.

## Procedure

### Step 1, find out what is actually being asked

The stated question is often not the real one. "Should I learn a new framework" sometimes means "I am
bored", "I am underpaid", or "I am afraid of being replaced". Those need different answers.

Ask what changed recently. Career questions usually arrive because something moved: a bad review, a
colleague's promotion, a layoff round, a birthday, a new baby.

### Step 2, inventory the non-transferable assets

This is what makes the answer theirs. Most of it never appears on a CV.

Domain knowledge from unglamorous work, meaning the industries they understand from the inside. Someone
who spent four years in insurance claims processing and can write software has a combination most people
cannot buy.

Relationships. Who would take their call, hire them, or refer them today.

Credentials with real gate value, meaning licences and certifications that legally or practically
restrict who can do the work.

Geography and language, including markets they can serve that others cannot reach.

Tolerance. What they can stand that most people cannot, including on-call, travel, conflict, sales,
regulatory detail, or long feedback cycles.

Financial position, which determines how much risk is available to them. This is the input that most
changes the answer and the one most often left out.

Then ask the question that decides most of it: what combination do they have that is rare? Value
concentrates in combinations, not in single skills, because single skills have many suppliers.

### Step 3, define the paths concretely

Vague paths cannot be compared. Turn each option into something with a duration, a cost, and a first
concrete step.

For each path state the time to first income at the new level, the money required or forgone, what has to
be learned and roughly how long that takes, the probability of it working based on retrieved evidence
rather than optimism, and what it closes off.

### Step 4, run the numbers

Compute the crossover point. If a path costs two years of reduced income to reach a higher level, when
does cumulative earnings overtake staying? Often the answer is longer than people assume, and sometimes
it never does, which is still fine if the reason is not money.

Use retrieved salary data for their country, level, and city. Label it with its date, since pay data goes
stale quickly.

Check survivability. Can they reach the other side without running out of money? A plan that is correct
and unaffordable is not a plan.

### Step 5, apply the swap test

Would this recommendation be equally good advice for someone with a different financial position, city,
and background? If yes, it is generic and has to be redone using the inventory.

### Step 6, decide

Give one recommendation, the strongest argument against it, and the conditions that would change it.

Then a review date and a measurable checkpoint, so the decision does not get silently abandoned or
silently extended.

```
RECOMMENDATION
[One path, in one or two sentences.]

WHY THIS ONE FOR YOU
[The specific assets it uses. Must fail the swap test.]

WHAT IT COSTS
[Years, money, and what gets closed off.]

STRONGEST ARGUMENT AGAINST
[The real one, not a token objection.]

WHAT EVERYONE ELSE WAS TOLD
[The standard advice, and why it is not the answer here.]

FIRST STEP THIS WEEK
[One concrete action.]

REVIEW ON [date]
[The measurable checkpoint, and what a miss means.]
```

## Traps worth naming

Sunk cost in a degree or a decade. Time already spent is not an argument for spending more.

Prestige substituting for outcome. A recognisable employer is worth something in the next job search and
nothing if the work does not develop anything transferable.

Optimising for interview performance rather than for the work, which produces a career of starts.

Avoiding all commercial skill. The people who negotiate, write, and sell get paid more for the same
technical work, and this remains true across fields.

Confusing a hobby with a career, which is legitimate as a choice and expensive as a mistake. Say which one
it is.

Waiting for certainty. Most career information only arrives after the commitment, which is why the review
date matters more than the initial confidence.

## Boundaries

This is not legal, immigration, tax, or financial advice. Where a decision depends on visa status,
pension treatment, or tax residency, say that a qualified professional in the relevant jurisdiction has to
confirm it, and say which specific question to put to them.

## Self-audit

- The recommendation fails the swap test, meaning it would be wrong advice for someone with a different financial position and background.
- Runway in months was established from savings and costs before any path was priced.
- Every salary or hiring figure carries a source and a date, or is labelled as an assumption with a way to check it.
- The trade is stated in years and money, not in encouragement.
- The strongest argument against the recommendation is a real one, not a token objection.
- The output ends with one decision, a first step this week, and a review date.

## When to stay off

If the user says "stop" or "just execute" they want the decision, not the analysis. Give the single
recommendation and the first step, and drop the inventory, the crossover maths, and the traps list. This
off switch stays off for the rest of the session unless the user asks to reopen the reasoning. Advice
that keeps arriving after the person has chosen becomes nagging.

## Honest limits

This skill reasons about the trade a career move makes in time and money. It does not give legal,
immigration, tax, or financial advice, and it cannot tell the user whether a visa will be granted, how a
pension transfers across a border, or what a move does to their tax residency. Put those to a qualified
professional in the relevant jurisdiction as a specific question, such as "does leaving employment for
self-employment change my right to remain on this visa", and act only on their answer.

The numbers it uses are retrieved and dated, not forecast, and salary data goes stale within a year. Where
the decision turns on the arithmetic of an offer, the runway, or the crossover point, hand the figures to
`business-model` for the modelling and to `finance-books` for anything touching bookkeeping or accounts.
Running the actual search, the CV, and the negotiation belongs to `job-hunt` once the direction is set.
Worked path comparisons are in `references/path-comparison.md`, and the asset inventory prompts are in
`references/asset-inventory.md`.
