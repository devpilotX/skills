# Forms, pricing, and measuring a change

The two places a converting page leaks are the form and the moment someone calls a change a winner too early. Both have concrete rules.

## Form friction

Every field lowers completion, so the count of fields is a cost paid in conversions. The rule of thumb: ask only for what the very next step needs, and collect the rest after the person has converted.

Email alone is often the strongest first ask, because it lets a relationship start and everything else can be gathered later. Adding name and company can cut completion noticeably for a top-of-funnel offer, and the data is rarely worth the loss at that stage.

A phone number before any relationship exists is the single most common leak. People guard their phone number far more than their email, and asking for it early signals a sales call they did not agree to. Defer it until after the person has said yes to something.

Do not pre-check an upsell, a newsletter opt-in, or a consent box. A pre-checked box that costs the visitor money or subscribes them without a clear choice is a dark pattern, it is unlawful in several jurisdictions, and it is refused under the skill's non-negotiables even when asked.

Label fields for what they are and why they are needed. A field labeled "work email so we can send your login" completes better than a bare "email", because it answers the visitor's silent "why do you want this".

## Pricing presentation

Show the price. A visitor who is ready to buy and cannot find the price has to stop and hunt, and many will simply leave instead. Hide pricing only when the deal genuinely requires negotiation, such as enterprise contracts with custom terms.

Anchor with the plan most buyers should choose and mark it visually so the eye lands there first. Three tiers is a common shape because it gives a cheap option, a default, and a premium that makes the default look reasonable. More than four tiers usually means the buyer has to do work the page should have done.

Name each tier by who it is for, not only by feature count. "For solo developers", "for a growing team", "for a company with compliance needs" lets a visitor self-select in one read. A wall of feature checkmarks makes them compare instead of choose.

State the billing period next to the price. If the large number is the annual-equivalent monthly price, say so plainly, because a visitor who expects to be charged the small monthly number and sees the annual total on their card feels tricked, and that feeling drives refunds and chargebacks.

## Measuring a change before believing it

The trap is calling a new version a winner after a handful of conversions. Small samples swing wildly, and a version that looks 30 percent better after 20 visitors often reverts to no difference after 2000.

Pick one primary metric that is the real action: completed signups or completed purchases, not clicks on the button. A click is a proxy, and optimizing a proxy can lower the thing you actually want.

Before changing anything, write down the current conversion rate. Without a baseline there is nothing to compare against, and after the change you will not know whether it helped.

Estimate the sample size before starting, not after. As a rough rule of thumb, detecting a small relative improvement, say a lift from 5 percent to 6 percent, needs on the order of several thousand visitors per variant to be reliable. The exact number depends on the baseline rate and the size of the change you care about; a smaller change needs a much larger sample. Use a sample-size calculator or a statistician for anything the business will bet on. This is a first-read rule of thumb, not a statistical guarantee.

Do not stop the test the moment it crosses significance. Peeking repeatedly and stopping at the first good-looking moment inflates false positives badly. Decide the sample size and the run length in advance, then wait for it.

Run the test long enough to cover the natural cycle of the traffic. A test that runs Tuesday to Thursday misses weekend behavior, and buyers often differ by day of week. A full week, and often two, is the honest minimum for most consumer traffic.
