# Calibration

This file quotes the disclaimers and chat pleasantries it tells you to remove, so it declares linter
exemptions on the line above.

Read this before telling anyone their writing looks machine generated, and before stripping a document
down to nothing in the name of cleaning it. Both mistakes cost more than the tells do.

## The accusation problem

Untrained human judgement performs close to chance at separating machine text from human text, and
classifier tools have error rates high enough that Wikipedia instructs its own editors not to rely on
them. A false accusation drives away the person who wrote the thing and poisons the next conversation.

So do not say "this is AI". Point at specific patterns and ask. "Three participial tails and four tier
one words in two paragraphs" is a checkable observation. "This reads like ChatGPT" is an insult with no
evidence attached.

When you are the one writing, the same asymmetry applies in reverse: passing a style check is not
evidence of authorship, and it never will be.

## Not evidence, however much it feels like it

Perfect grammar. Many people write correctly for a living. Clean prose is the expected output of an
editor, not a confession.

A mix of casual and clinical register in one piece. This is what a specialist sounds like writing
outside a journal. It also appears in writing by young authors, by neurodivergent authors, and in any
document several people edited.

Prose that strikes you as bland or robotic. Model output has specific traits and skews positive and
verbose; a reader unfamiliar with those traits often labels ordinary flat writing as robotic and misses
actual machine text entirely.

Formal, academic or elaborate vocabulary. The overused set is a specific list of words, and the
correlation does not extend to long words in general.

A transition word on its own. A handful of openers are genuinely overrepresented, and essay writing has
used them for a century. One `Additionally` is nothing. A page opening six paragraphs that way is
something.

Missing citations. Hundreds of thousands of Wikipedia articles carry citation-needed tags that predate
any chatbot, and modern assistants add citations routinely. Their citations are often wrong, which is a
different finding, checked a different way.

Strange or broken markup. Misplaced tags and mangled syntax more often come from a browser extension, a
translation tool, or a visual editor than from a model. Correct markup is equally uninformative, since
a preview button exists.

## Markers that dated fast

These were common in older output and are now rare. They are useful for identifying old text, and
useless for judging anything written recently.

A didactic disclaimer aimed at an imagined reader, warning that a topic is sensitive or varies by
jurisdiction, often opening with a phrase about how something is important to note or important to
remember.

A closing section named Conclusion, or a paragraph that restates what the section just said.

A refusal or partial refusal left in the text, including any sentence in which the writer describes
itself as an AI language model before offering an alternative.

Text that stops mid sentence, which happened when a response hit a length limit. A bad copy and paste
produces the same thing, and so does a copyright removal.

Access dates on citations that sit oddly far in the past relative to when the text was written.

Synonym rotation to avoid repeating a word, which came from a repetition penalty. Repeating the right
word is the human move.

## Overcorrection has its own smell

A document with zero em dashes anywhere, no transitions at all, every sentence clipped to eight words,
all structure flattened into prose, and contractions forced into a formal report reads as processed
rather than as written. Balanced writing uses `however` sometimes and a list sometimes.

Match the genre instead of applying one rule everywhere. A README should have bullet lists and code
fences. A recipe should be numbered. A changelog is a list by definition. Applying encyclopedic prose
rules to any of those produces something equally artificial, just in a different direction.

The failure mode to watch for in yourself: paraphrasing around a rule while keeping the empty claim.
Rewriting "underscores the importance of transparency" as "shows how important transparency is" scores
better and says exactly as little.

## Where the line is

Removing the markers of machine writing changes how text reads. It does not change where the text came
from, and it is not a disclosure mechanism.

Where a venue requires disclosure of machine assistance, disclose it. That includes academic
submission, journalism with a stated policy, and Wikipedia itself, which treats undisclosed generated
content as a policy problem regardless of how clean the prose is.

If someone asks for help concealing machine assistance where disclosure is required, say plainly that
you will not, then help them write well and disclose.
