# Synthesis that survives a sceptic

Interview notes become a decision through synthesis, and synthesis is where confirmation bias does its
worst work. The method below makes the findings defensible against someone who wanted the opposite
conclusion.

## The core discipline: observed against inferred

Every line in a synthesis is one of two things.

An observation is what a participant said or did, in their words or your faithful record. "Participant
4 keeps a spreadsheet of orders and updates it every morning." This is a fact about the interview.

An inference is what you concluded from it. "Users want a dashboard." This is your interpretation, and
it may be wrong.

Mark each one. An inference recorded as an observation becomes a false fact the team treats as ground
truth, and once the two are mixed you cannot separate them again. The single most common synthesis
failure is promoting an inference to an observation without noticing.

## Building patterns from instances

Group observations that repeat across participants into a pattern. A pattern needs instances behind it,
cited by participant.

State the count. "Six of nine participants maintained a manual tracker" is a pattern. "Users maintain
manual trackers" hides how many, and a reader cannot judge its weight.

State the counter count too. How many participants did not show the pattern matters as much as how many
did. A pattern seen in six of nine is different from six of six, and hiding the three who differed
overstates the finding.

Quote the words. A pattern backed by the participants' own phrases is far harder to dismiss than one
stated in your paraphrase, because the reader can check your interpretation against the source.

One loud participant is not a pattern. A single vivid story is memorable and pulls synthesis toward
itself out of proportion to its weight. Weight by how many participants showed a thing, not by how
strongly one of them said it.

## The sceptic test

Before you present, attack your own synthesis as someone who wanted the opposite answer.

Take each finding and ask: could a sceptic find the opposite in these same notes. If the notes support
both readings, the finding is not established and you say so.

Check for the questions you did not ask. A pattern you only found because you asked leading questions is
an artifact of the interview, not a finding about users.

Check the sample. If every participant came from one source, the pattern may be about that source
rather than about the problem. Name the limit.

Check disconfirming cases explicitly. Find the participants who did not fit the pattern and account for
them. A synthesis that ignores the cases against it is advocacy, not research.

## Saturation and when to stop

Saturation is the point where new conversations stop producing new patterns. It is the honest signal to
stop, not a fixed count.

A rule of thumb from usability practice is that five to eight conversations per distinct segment surface
most recurring patterns, and beyond a dozen in one segment you are usually confirming rather than
learning. These are starting points, not laws.

If patterns keep shifting past a dozen in what you thought was one segment, the segment is actually
several mixed together. Split it and interview each separately, because a pattern averaged across
distinct segments describes nobody.

A narrow, concrete question saturates fast. A broad, exploratory question about a diverse population
needs more conversations. Let the incoming patterns tell you when to stop rather than a target number.

## When research gets misused

Ratification. The decision is already made and the research is run to justify it. The tell: no finding
would change the plan. The result is corrupted from the start, because the team hears what confirms the
plan and discounts the rest, and it teaches everyone that research is theatre.

The fix: before recruiting, write what each finding would change. If the honest answer is nothing, do
not run it. Say the decision is being made on conviction and skip the pretence of evidence.

Cherry picking. Presenting the three quotes that support the plan and omitting the seven that did not.
The fix: report counts, not quotes alone, and show the counter cases.

Over reach. Turning a qualitative finding into a number. "Most users want this" from nine interviews is
not a measurement. A qualitative study finds what problems exist and why; it cannot tell you how many
people have them. Quantifying needs analytics or a properly sampled survey.
