# Asking questions that get truth

The difference between a useful interview and a comfortable one is the question. Below are the shapes to
use, the shapes to avoid, and why each fails.

## The three questions that lie

The leading question. It names the answer and the respondent obliges. "Would a faster export save you a
lot of time?" gets a yes because you signalled you want one. The fix: ask about the behaviour without
the judgement. "Tell me about the last time you exported something."

The hypothetical. It asks the respondent to predict their own behaviour, which people do badly. "Would
you pay for this?" and "would you use a feature that did X?" get optimistic answers that do not survive
contact with a real choice. The fix: ask what they do now and what they have paid for before. Past
behaviour predicts future behaviour better than stated intention.

The general habit. It invites the respondent to describe an idealised version of themselves. "How often
do you review your metrics?" gets the aspirational answer, not the real one. The fix: anchor to the
last real instance. "When did you last look at your metrics, and what made you look then?"

## The shapes that work

The last concrete instance. "Walk me through the last time this happened." A specific recent event has
detail the respondent cannot invent, and the detail is where the truth sits.

The story follow. Once they start a story, follow it rather than returning to your list. "What happened
next?" "Why did you do it that way?" "What were you trying to get done?" The unscripted branches surface
what you did not know to ask.

The five whys, used gently. When someone states a want, ask why, then why again, until you reach the
underlying job. The stated want is often a solution the respondent guessed at; the job beneath it is the
real target.

The silence. After an answer, wait. People fill silence with the qualification or the real reason they
held back. Rushing to the next question cuts off the most honest part.

The contradiction, surfaced kindly. When a stated preference and a described behaviour disagree, name it
without accusation. "Earlier you said X, but last week you did Y. Help me understand." The gap is the
finding.

## Preferences against behaviour

What people say they want and what they do diverge constantly, and the divergence is data, not noise.

Stated preference is cheap. Agreeing that a feature sounds good costs the respondent nothing, so the
answer carries little weight.

Revealed behaviour is expensive. Building a workaround, paying for a tool, or spending an hour a week on
a manual step costs real effort, and effort spent is a strong signal of a real need.

The rule: when a preference and a behaviour disagree, believe the behaviour. When you only have a
preference, discount it until behaviour confirms it.

## Running the conversation

Start broad and specific at once: broad topic, specific instance. "I want to understand how you handle
X. Tell me about the last time you did it."

Do not pitch. The moment you describe your solution, the respondent switches from telling you their
experience to reacting to your idea, and reactions to an idea are weak data. Save any concept for the
end, and frame the reaction honestly.

Do not defend. When a respondent criticises something, resist explaining why it is that way. The
criticism is the gift; a defence stops it.

Record words, not summaries, during the conversation. A paraphrase written in the moment bakes in your
interpretation and you cannot recover the original later.

Keep it to the time you promised. A rushed respondent at the end gives short answers, and the last
questions are often the ones that mattered most.

## A screener that filters for the problem

The screener decides your sample, so it filters on behaviour rather than interest.

Bad screener: "Are you interested in tools for X?" Everyone curious says yes and your sample is people
who like the topic, not people who have the problem.

Good screener: "In the last month, how many times did you do X?" A behaviour question with a number
sorts the people who actually have the problem from the people who find it interesting. Set the
threshold before you send it, and hold to it even when recruiting is slow, because letting in people
below the bar quietly poisons the findings.
