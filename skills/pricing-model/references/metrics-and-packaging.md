# Pricing metrics and packaging

How to choose the thing the price scales with, and how to arrange tiers so the packaging sells rather
than confuses.

## What makes a good pricing metric

The price scales on one metric. A good one has three properties at once.

It tracks value received. As the buyer gets more benefit, the metric rises, so the bill rises with the
value and not ahead of it. A seat count works when each user gets value. A usage count works when each
unit of usage does work for the buyer.

It is predictable to the buyer. A buyer who cannot forecast next month's bill within reason will not
sign, or will churn when a spike arrives. A metric that swings wildly with factors the buyer does not
control fails here.

It resists gaming without heavy policing. If a buyer can get the same value while keeping the metric
low, they will, and you will spend effort enforcing definitions. Prefer a metric the buyer cannot
easily suppress without giving up the value.

## Common metrics and where they fit

Per seat. Fits when value grows with the number of people using the product. Simple to understand and
predict. Weakness: buyers share logins to suppress the count, and value sometimes comes from a few
heavy users rather than the headcount.

Per usage, such as requests, gigabytes, or transactions. Fits when value tracks volume closely. Scales
cleanly with heavy users. Weakness: unpredictable bills scare buyers, and a surprise invoice is a
common cancellation trigger. Usage pricing usually needs a cap, a prepaid bucket, or an alert.

Per outcome, such as a percentage of revenue processed or leads generated. Aligns price to value most
directly. Weakness: hard to measure cleanly, and the buyer resents paying more when their own effort
drove the outcome.

Flat per account. Simplest to sell and predict. Weakness: your heaviest users are your least
profitable, and you cannot grow revenue within an account without a new sale.

Tiered by a feature set. Fits when segments want different capabilities rather than different volume.
Weakness: buyers game the boundary, and a feature that many need in a high tier reads as a penalty.

Most durable pricing blends a base fee for predictability with a usage component for expansion, so the
buyer has a floor they can forecast and the vendor has upside as value grows.

## The expansion test

A pricing metric passes the expansion test if a customer who succeeds with the product naturally pays
more over time without a new negotiation. Run the test: picture your happiest customer a year in, using
the product twice as much. Does the bill rise smoothly, jump at a cliff, or stay flat while they take
more value. A flat answer caps your growth. A cliff drives churn at the boundary. A smooth rise is what
you want.

## Packaging into tiers

Three tiers is a default, not a rule. It works because it gives a low anchor, a target middle, and a
high option that frames the middle as reasonable, an effect where the presence of an expensive option
raises what the middle feels worth. Decide the count from your segments: two if buyers split cleanly in
two, four if there are four real segments, one if the product is genuinely one thing.

Place the upgrade driver at the boundary. The single feature or limit that pushes a buyer from one tier
to the next is the lever, so put the most wanted upgrade right at the line. Do not scatter must have
features across tiers, which forces buyers to over buy and breeds resentment.

Name tiers by the buyer they fit, not by adjectives. A tier called by the segment it serves helps the
buyer self select. A tier called by a metal or a size makes them guess.

Anchor high. The presence of a high tier, even one few buy, lifts what the middle tier feels worth.
Removing the top option often drops middle tier sales, because the middle no longer looks like the
sensible choice.

## Free tiers and trials

A free tier is a marketing cost, so it needs a job: acquisition, a viral loop, or a foot in the door to
a paying team. A free tier with no path to paid is a cost with no return. Set the limit at the point
where a buyer getting real value has a clear reason to upgrade, not so generous that the paid tier adds
nothing and not so mean that the free tier never shows value.

A time boxed trial suits products whose value is obvious within the window. A usage limited free tier
suits products whose value grows with adoption over a longer arc. Match the model to how fast the
product proves itself.
