# Getting the specifics out of the writer

Most drafts read generic because the writer handed over a topic and no material. The fix is not better sentences; it is better questions. Use these when the writer says "write about X" and X is a category rather than a story.

## Why generic happens

A model fills gaps with the most probable sentence, and the most probable sentence about any product is the one that fits every product. "We help teams work better" is the average of a million about-pages. The only cure is a detail that lowers the probability: a number, a name, a moment. Those live in the writer's head, not the training data, so they have to be asked for.

## The extraction interview

Ask these in order. Stop when you have at least three answers a competitor could not have given. Keep each question narrow, because broad questions get broad answers.

1. What happened right before you decided to write this. Point to the ticket, the meeting, the complaint, or the win that made today the day. This gives the piece a reason to exist now.
2. Give me one real number. How many customers, how many milliseconds, how much it cost, how long it took. A range is fine if the exact figure is sensitive. Round numbers that sound invented are worse than an honest range.
3. Name one specific person or case, disguised if needed. "A fintech in Berlin with eight engineers" beats "our customers". One named case carries more than ten abstractions.
4. What did you get wrong first. The mistake and its cost is the most trusted content a writer owns, because no competitor will volunteer theirs.
5. What would a skeptic in your audience say to this. Their strongest objection, stated in their words. Answering it in the piece is what earns the reader who was about to close the tab.
6. What is the one thing you know that your competitors either do not know or will not say. This is the angle. If the answer is nothing, the piece has no reason to be read over any other.

## When the writer cannot answer

If the writer has no number, no case, and no mistake, do not paper over it with confident abstraction. Say plainly that the piece will read generic without at least one concrete input, and offer the smallest possible ask: one number, or one sentence a real customer said. A short piece built on one true specific beats a long piece built on none.

If the specifics are genuinely unavailable, for example a pre-launch product with no users, narrow the job of the piece to what can be made concrete: the exact problem observed, the design decision and its tradeoff, a walkthrough of the thing actually built. A product with no customers still has a real reason it was built, and that reason is a specific.

## Turning answers into sentences

A raw answer is not a sentence. "About 200ms cold start" becomes a claim the reader can check: "On the free tier a cold start finishes under 200 milliseconds, measured from the first request after an idle hour." Attach the condition, because a number without its condition reads as marketing and invites disbelief.

Keep the disguise honest. Changing a customer's country to protect them is fine. Inventing a customer is not, and a reader who catches one invented detail discounts every other detail in the piece.
